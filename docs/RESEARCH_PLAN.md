# Market and Customer Research Plan

**Status:** Ready to execute  
**Purpose:** Decide whether and where an AI Workspace should enter the market.

## Research questions

### Customer and problem
- Which role experiences the problem directly?
- What job or business outcome are they trying to achieve?
- How do they solve it today: people, spreadsheets, SaaS, scripts, AI assistants, or agencies?
- How often does the workflow occur?
- What is the cost of delay, errors, coordination, or manual review?
- Who owns the budget and approves a purchase?

### Alternatives and competition
- Which direct products offer agent/workflow orchestration?
- Which adjacent products solve the job through automation, project management, CRM, or AI copilots?
- What do customers actually use, and what do they still do manually?
- What are the pricing model, target segment, deployment model, and limitations?
- Is the unmet need genuinely underserved, or merely difficult to market?

### Feasibility and differentiation
- Can a narrow workflow be delivered with existing models and integrations?
- Does it require multiple agents, or would one agent plus deterministic workflow rules be more reliable?
- What permissions, audit logs, data handling, and approvals are necessary?
- What measurable advantage could make a customer switch or pay?

## Evidence hierarchy

Prioritize evidence in roughly this order:
1. Observed workflow, real artifacts, and measured operational costs
2. Customer interviews describing recent specific behavior
3. Paid pilots, pre-orders, or a concrete commitment to test
4. Product documentation, pricing pages, case studies, and credible benchmarks
5. Independent institutional research with transparent methods
6. Expert commentary and community discussions
7. Our own assumptions

A large market forecast is context, not validation of a specific product or willingness to pay.

## Research method

1. Generate 5–8 candidate workflows across plausible customer segments.
2. For each, define the user, trigger, inputs, current process, bottleneck, outcome, frequency, and buyer.
3. Map direct competitors and substitutes, including manual work.
4. Record sources with publication date, URL, relevant claim, and limitations.
5. Interview prospective users about their last real instance of the workflow; avoid leading questions.
6. Estimate baseline time/cost and the improvement needed to justify purchase.
7. Rank opportunities using the scorecard below.
8. Test the top candidate with a concierge/manual prototype before building software.
9. Record evidence that would disprove the preferred idea.

## Candidate scorecard

Score each dimension from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong). Add evidence and confidence; do not let a numeric score disguise missing data.

- Pain severity
- Frequency of the workflow
- Cost or revenue impact
- Clear budget owner
- Reachability of initial customers
- Weakness of current alternatives
- Time to demonstrate value
- Technical feasibility
- Reliability and risk profile
- Expansion potential into adjacent workflows

Do not compare scores without noting evidence quality and confidence. A high expansion score cannot compensate for an unvalidated core problem.

## Interview prompts

- Walk me through the last time you completed this workflow.
- What triggered it, and what happened step by step?
- Which tools and people were involved?
- Where did the work stall or need correction?
- How much time or money did it consume?
- What happens if it is late or incorrect?
- Have you tried to solve this before? What did you pay for?
- Who would approve a solution and from which budget?
- What would make you refuse to use an automated version?

Avoid asking only whether someone “likes the idea.” Compliments are weaker evidence than behavior, budget, access to a pilot, or payment.

## Research output requirements

Each opportunity brief should contain:
- User and buyer
- Job-to-be-done and current workflow
- Quantified pain estimate or clearly marked unknowns
- Alternatives and competitor evidence
- Proposed wedge and why it may be better
- Risks, disconfirming evidence, and open questions
- Pilot design and success/failure thresholds
- Confidence level and next action
